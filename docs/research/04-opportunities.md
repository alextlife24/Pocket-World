# Pocket World — Stage 4: Concept Generation and Ranking

## Scoring method

Scores are comparative product judgments, not market measurements. Each criterion is 1–10. The weighted score is:

$$
0.12O + 0.12A + 0.12U + 0.15E + 0.13R + 0.12S + 0.09F + 0.07M + 0.04X + 0.04V
$$

| Criterion | Weight | Reason |
|---|---:|---|
| Originality (O) | 12% | “Cute tiny world” alone is crowded. |
| Immediate appeal (A) | 12% | The click must beat browser abandonment. |
| 30-second understandability (U) | 12% | No install/store/tutorial can carry the concept. |
| Emotional attachment (E) | 15% | Central promise; highest weight. |
| Retention (R) | 13% | Must survive past web-toy novelty. |
| Shareability (S) | 12% | Required growth mechanism for a URL product. |
| Development feasibility (F) | 9% | Small independent project constraint. |
| Mobile suitability (M) | 7% | Primary device constraint. |
| Long-term expansion (X) | 4% | Valuable only after the core loop works. |
| Visual identity (V) | 4% | Important, but art cannot rescue a weak loop. |
| **Total** | **100%** | |

## Twelve possible Pocket Worlds

### C1 — Living Memory Postcard Village

A one-screen village with three residents. Once per day, one resident presents a small dilemma. The player chooses by tapping the world; the choice creates an immediate physical change, later resident behavior, and a standardized share postcard. Previous decisions change future options.

**Core test:** Does visible remembered consequence create attachment?

### C2 — Weather Jar Ecosystem

A glass-jar ecosystem receives one daily weather choice—rain, sun, fog, wind. Plants and creatures adapt over time; the jar generates a timelapse postcard.

**Core test:** Is environmental authorship emotionally strong without named characters?

### C3 — One Resident Diary

One highly expressive resident leaves a short daily note and asks for one decision. Its room, routines, language, and relationship change over seven days.

**Core test:** Can one character achieve attachment faster and cheaper than a world simulation?

### C4 — Daily Mystery Diorama

Each day, a small unexplained object or event appears. The player inspects three clues and chooses an interpretation; the world reveals the consequence tomorrow.

**Core test:** Does mystery create return motivation without becoming a content treadmill?

### C5 — Procedural Island Gardener

The player places one seed/object per day; an authored growth grammar turns choices into a unique island composition. Residents are ambient rather than central.

**Core test:** Is effortless visual creation enough to sustain a daily ritual?

### C6 — Tiny Civilization Oracle

A civilization asks the player one policy question daily; dozens of autonomous citizens adapt, form factions, and build districts.

**Core test:** Can large-scale emergence remain readable and cozy?

### C7 — AI Resident Chatroom

Three AI-driven residents talk to each other and remember the player’s free-text conversations. The village reflects topics discussed.

**Core test:** Does conversational freedom justify runtime cost, safety, privacy, and consistency risk?

### C8 — Pocket Time Capsule

The player records one tiny, non-sensitive in-fiction choice or mood token daily. Seven tokens become a visual weekly capsule and a resident-written summary.

**Core test:** Can ritualized reflection create attachment without being a wellness app?

### C9 — Collectible Seed Worlds

Each session begins with a seed containing a rule combination; nurturing reveals a miniature biome. Players collect, display, and share seed codes.

**Core test:** Does collection strengthen or dilute ownership of one persistent world?

### C10 — Shared Neighbor Windows

Each player has one private room/world. Friends appear as windows at its edges; once per day, a silent visitor can leave a small object or gesture.

**Core test:** Can intimate asynchronous social interaction work without chat/feed moderation?

### C11 — Mood Mirror Habitat

The player selects one abstract feeling color; weather, sound, creatures, and resident behavior transform in response, creating a calming visual log.

**Core test:** Is emotional resonance worth the privacy/health-positioning risk?

### C12 — Disaster-and-Recovery Terrarium

Gentle disruptions—storm, fallen tree, lost parcel—change the terrarium. The player chooses how residents rebuild, leaving scars and traditions instead of resetting.

**Core test:** Can low-stakes adversity create stronger stories while remaining cozy?

## Ranking rationale and evidence connection

- The top concept combines **daily restraint** (Wordle), **delayed visit/reveal** (Neko Atsume), **personal place and residents** (Animal Crossing), and **automatically attractive output** (Tiny Glade/Townscaper).[9][10][15]
- The AI Chatroom scores poorly on feasibility because current generative-character products expose variable model/credit costs, while AI Dungeon’s history illustrates coherence, moderation, and privacy surfaces.[28][35]
- The Tiny Civilization concept is expansion-friendly but fails the “simulate only what the player can perceive” scope test; WorldBox demonstrates the attraction of civilization emergence, but also the difference between a full sandbox and a one-screen short-session product.[16]
- Weather Jar is visually strong and feasible but lacks the named-person continuity seen in Neko Atsume, Finch, and Animal Crossing.[9][10][11]
- Time Capsule is cheap and mobile-friendly, but risks becoming a mood tracker with game decoration rather than a game.

## TOP 3 CONCEPTS

### 1. Living Memory Postcard Village — 8.62

**Why it ranks first:** It directly tests the project’s most defensible combination: meaningful daily choice + visible persistent divergence + resident memory + social artifact. Each element can be implemented through authored state, and the same event can produce comparable but personal postcards.

**Failure condition:** If players perceive the outcomes as cosmetic or the residents as scripted, the concept loses both attachment and uniqueness.

### 2. One Resident Diary — 8.02

**Why it ranks second:** Highest focus. One resident permits better animation, writing, and continuity at indie scope. It is the safest fallback if three-resident simulation is too expensive.

**Why it does not win:** The title “Pocket World” promises a place, not only a companion. A diary is also less visually divergent and less naturally shareable.

### 3. Pocket Time Capsule — 7.98

**Why it ranks third:** Very feasible, strong on mobile, and naturally accumulative. It could generate a beautiful seven-day artifact.

**Why it does not win:** The user’s real feelings could create privacy and wellness-positioning risks; abstract inputs may feel like journaling with cosmetics. The game should first prove attachment through in-fiction choices.

## ONE PRIMARY CONCEPT

# Living Memory Postcard Village

### Product definition

A player owns a one-screen village inhabited by three authored residents. On each real-world day, one resident presents a 10–20 second situation with two gentle trade-offs. The player chooses by touching a location/object. The world transforms immediately; during the absence, residents complete the change. On return, a concise recap shows what happened and how one resident remembers it. Every result can become a spoiler-light postcard linked to a read-only snapshot.

### Why this concept has the strongest chance

1. **It has a click hook:** “Make one choice; tomorrow your village remembers.”
2. **It pays off within 30 seconds:** the first choice changes the world immediately.
3. **It has a natural tomorrow hook:** an unfinished resident action creates a delayed reveal.
4. **It has a seven-day ownership hook:** seven decisions leave seven inspectable marks.
5. **It has a share reason:** the same daily situation yields visibly different, comparable outcomes.
6. **It is technically bounded:** three residents, one scene, binary events, tagged memories, deterministic elapsed-time simulation.
7. **It avoids false AI necessity:** ordinary game state can deliver continuity more reliably and cheaply.
8. **It can fail cheaply:** a seven-event prototype with static art can test the core emotional claim before a full engine.

### Required modification to the original concept

Do **not** promise a fully autonomous AI society. Promise a **small authored world that remembers consequential choices**. “Living” should mean readable routines, elapsed-time change, and historical consequences—not continuous server simulation or unrestricted generation.
